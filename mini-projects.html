01 · GAME MODIFICATION

🌻 Plants vs. Zombies

Experimenting with plant cooldowns and resource requirements.

BEFORE
AFTER

What I did

I experimented with bypassing the normal plant cooldown and resource requirements to see how the game's behaviour changed.

As you can see in the before video, each plant has its own cost and cooldown, meaning I couldn't immediately place the same plant again after using it.

In the after video, all of the plant costs have been reduced to 0, so the player is no longer charged for placing plants. The cooldowns have also been removed, allowing plants to be placed continuously.

What I learned

This experiment gave me more experience investigating unfamiliar game behaviour and figuring out which values were responsible for specific mechanics.

I also learned that changing one value does not always explain everything that is happening. I had to compare different values and repeatedly test my changes to understand what was actually controlling the cooldown and plant cost.

It was a good exercise in being patient and curious when I don't immediately know how something works.

02 · GAME MODIFICATION

🍕 Good Pizza, Great Pizza

Tracing the game's money handling and experimenting with its purchasing behaviour.

BEFORE
AFTER

What I did

I spent a lot of time tracing the function responsible for subtracting money when making a purchase. After finding it, I experimented with changing its behaviour.

In the before video, you can see that trying to buy something that costs more than my available money triggers a "Not enough funds" popup. Both my money and gems also decrease when making a purchase.

In the after video, I can make purchases even when I don't have enough initial funds. On top of that, both my money and gems increase instead of decreasing.

What I learned

This experiment taught me a lot about persistence when investigating unfamiliar code. Finding the relevant function took considerably more time than I initially expected.

I also became more comfortable following values through the program and testing different possibilities instead of immediately knowing which address or function I needed.

Most importantly, it showed me how much you can learn simply by being willing to investigate something you don't understand yet.

03 · GAME MODIFICATION

🛒 Supermarket Simulator 3D

Experimenting with how the game handles money and energy.

BEFORE
AFTER

What I did

I experimented with the game's resource handling and modified the behaviour of its money and energy values.

In the before video, you can see that the game starts with only $40. If there isn't enough money for a purchase, the game displays a "Not enough funds" notification and the basket doesn't empty. Spending energy to make packages arrive faster also decreases the available energy.

In the after video, the initial amount of money no longer matters. Instead, my money increases when I spend it, and my energy also increases whenever I use it.

What I learned

This experiment gave me more practice identifying values that change during normal gameplay and figuring out what happens when their behaviour is modified.

It was especially interesting to see that changing the direction of a value update could affect the game in a completely different way. Instead of resources being consumed, they could effectively be generated by the same action.

It also gave me more experience testing changes in small steps and observing the result before moving on to something else.

04 · TRAINER

🧟 Dying Light Trainer

A small trainer created while experimenting with game memory and runtime modification.

SHOWCASE 01
SHOWCASE 02
SHOWCASE 03
SHOWCASE 04

What I made

I created a small trainer to experiment with modifying values in the game while it was running.

In the first showcase, you can see the effects of several trainer options. Normally, the grappling hook has a cooldown, dropped weapons disappear from my inventory, and my health decreases when I take damage. After enabling the corresponding options, the grappling hook no longer runs out, dropped weapons can be duplicated, and my health no longer decreases.

The second showcase demonstrates changing one weapon into another by selecting the weapon to change and then choosing the weapon it should become. This isn't something that can normally be done through the game's mechanics.

The third and fourth showcases focus on modifying individual weapon properties. I can change the weapon's rarity, durability, displayed quantity, and repair count. I can also add or remove upgrades and modifications from their respective slots, which normally cannot be changed after being added.

What I learned

This project gave me a lot more experience working with game memory and understanding how different values are represented and changed while a game is running.

I also learned how different pieces of a game can be connected. Changing something as simple as a weapon's quantity or durability meant figuring out which value controlled it and testing whether changing that value actually produced the behaviour I expected.

Building the trainer also taught me how much trial and error is involved in reverse engineering. A lot of the work was finding something interesting, testing it, breaking it, and then trying to understand why it behaved that way.

The trainer is still unfinished, but it was one of my more involved experiments and gave me a much better understanding of memory manipulation than I had before starting it.
